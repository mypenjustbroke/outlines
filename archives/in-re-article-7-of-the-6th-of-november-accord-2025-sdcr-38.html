In re Article 7 of the 6th of November Accord [2025] SDCR 38

MAJORITY OPINION by Chief Judge Muggy (With Judge Thyme and Judge Hmquestionable agreeing)

Introduction [1] This case comes before the Court due to a petition on the matter being submitted. In their petition, the petitioner outlines that an individual who was lawfully convicted of doxxing was able to speak in an interserver chat established in Article 7 of the Sixth of November Accord, and consequently, the rights outlined in Article 19 and Article 24 of the Constitution have been violated.

[2] The Court invited a response from the Attorney General’s Office, but they did not appoint a respondent.

[3] An injunction was granted on the suspension of the chat between SimDemocracy and Voices of Democracy, of which is now lifted.

Proactive, Reactive, and Retroactive Rights [4] In the enforcement of one’s rights, a Court must consider whether the right can proactively, reactively, or retroactively be enforced.

[4.1] A proactive right would be a right that the State can reasonably eliminate all of the ways in which it could be infringed upon so that the citizenry may exercise it. One such right would be the Freedom of Expression, wherein a citizen exercising their right to express themselves does not need to be responded to by the State because they have already taken the measures of which to make it so the citizen can exercise their right.

[4.2] A reactive right would be a right that must have precautionary measures as to not be violated, but typically need to be enforced after the State is made aware that a citizen(s) right has been infringed upon. An example of a reactive right would be the Right to Personal Safety, where when a citizen’s Right to Personal Safety is violated, the government must respond and uphold it, such as not placing a duty on an individual that may put their physical safety at risk after doing so.

[4.3] Retroactive rights would be rights that can be enforced on situations that occurred before the right’s enshrinement. As this Court is bound by Supreme Court precedent like [[Zepz367, ex parte Shun (Appellant) v State of SimDemocracy (Respondent) 2025 SDSC 39|Zepz367, ex parte Shun (Appellant) v State of SimDemocracy (Respondent) [2025] SDSC 39]], and one of the fundamental principles that govern SimDemocracy is that laws of the criminal variety may not be applied ex post facto, the Constitution and any right within it cannot be applied retroactively.

[5] The petitioner points to two (2) primary rights in order to argue for the chat’s unconstitutionality: Articles 19 and 24 of the Constitution, which are the Rights to Privacy and Personal Safety respectively.

[5.1] As briefly touched on in [4.2], the Right to Personal Safety is a reactive right, being that in the State cannot reasonably eliminate all possible ways that minors could be subjected to sexually explicit, graphically violent, or otherwise disturbing content, because the threat, however low it may be, is still ever-present. That does not necessarily exempt the State from being required to mitigate the possibilities as much as feasibly possible, but the only way to assuredly eliminate a point of exposure is to react to the content that violates that right accordingly. The same can be said for not placing any duty on a citizen that would put their physical safety at risk, because the citizen would have to express that their personal safety is being put at risk to a degree that is in violation to their rights.

[5.2] The Right to Privacy is much the same in the fact that the State can prescribe an organization to handle personal information and take the necessary steps to mitigate the possibility of infringing on the right itself, but that doesn’t change the fact that an infringement on the right cannot be completely ruled out by precautions, making the Right to Privacy a reactionary right.

Legal Validity and Enforcement of the Chat [6] Now that we’ve determined that the rights in which the petitioner is expressly worried about are reactive rights, we can apply the barometer of if the State has or can take reasonable measures as to ensure that most of the avenues in which those rights could be infringed upon have been mitigated via the legislation establishing the act and other pieces of legislation working in conjunction with it.

[6.1] To do this we must look at the treaty establishing the chat in the first place, being the Sixth of November Accord, and specifically Article 7 of that treaty, which states:

“Both governments shall take all necessary measures to establish this chat through the InterChat bot complying with all domestic and higher (TOS) regulation.”

[6.2] Given that this act explicitly states that the chat is to comply with all domestic and higher regulation, which includes SimDemocracy’s very own Constitution, Criminal Code, etc, it is safe to say that the State can take reasonable precaution to enforce the rights of the citizenry.

[7] Enforcing domestic law, though, would require the State to coordinate with Voices of Democracy that any individuals that have been sentenced to a legal ban from SimDemocracy and all its territories, be excluded from the chat, given that not doing so would both be against domestic law (lawful court sentencing) and possibly exposing the citizenry of SimDemocracy to an infringement on their rights.

[7.1] Other ways of enforcing domestic law will not be extrapolated by this Court as to allow for the executive to have their rightful authority to enforce the laws of SimDemocracy.

[8] As the State can reasonably mitigate most of the circumstances in which the rights of the citizenry be infringed upon through the language of the treaty and using other legislation, it is legally valid, but at any point where this may not be the case, the chat is to be immediately null and void.

Verdict [9] Article 7 and by extension the chat itself is legally valid insofar as the State can ensure that it can reasonably take measures to enforce domestic law and the rights of the citizenry.

Post-Script [10] While this opinion did not have to answer nearly as much as it did due to the fact that this Court could’ve simply dismissed the petitioner’s arguments, I am of the mind that the points brought up by a petition should be where a court, even an inquisitorial one, should mainly look towards when writing a verdict. As well as the fact that precedent elaborating on the peoples’ rights is generally good to have.
